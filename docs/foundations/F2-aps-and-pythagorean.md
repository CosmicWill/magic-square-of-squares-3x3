# F2 — Three-term APs of squares, Pythagorean decompositions, and the four-congrua reformulation

**Status: PROVEN** (classical ingredients included with complete proofs).
Verification: `python3 -m verify --only f2`.

[F1](F1-parametrization.md) reduced the problem to: make $c$, $c\pm u$,
$c\pm v$, $c\pm(u+v)$, $c\pm(u-v)$ nine distinct squares. Each $\pm$ pair
together with the center is a three-term arithmetic progression of squares.
This document pins down exactly which progressions exist, and turns the
problem into additive relations among *congrua* of a fixed hypotenuse.

## 1. APs of squares centered at a square

**Lemma F2.1 (parity).** If $p^2 + q^2 = 2m^2$ with $p,q \in \mathbb{Z}$,
then $p \equiv q \pmod 2$. *Proof.* $p^2+q^2$ is even, so $p, q$ have equal
parity. ∎

**Theorem F2.2 (the dictionary).** Fix $m \ge 1$. The maps

$$(p,q) \longmapsto \Big(e, f\Big) = \Big(\tfrac{q-p}{2},\, \tfrac{q+p}{2}\Big),
\qquad (e,f) \longmapsto (p,q) = (f-e,\, f+e)$$

are mutually inverse bijections between

$$\mathcal{A}(m) = \{(p,q) : 0 \le p \le q,\; p^2 + q^2 = 2m^2\}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\mathcal{P}(m) = \{(e,f) : 0 \le e \le f,\; e^2 + f^2 = m^2\},$$

and under this bijection the AP $(p^2, m^2, q^2)$ has common difference

$$d \;=\; m^2 - p^2 \;=\; q^2 - m^2 \;=\; 2ef.$$

*Proof.* If $p^2+q^2 = 2m^2$ then by F2.1 $e = (q-p)/2$, $f = (q+p)/2$ are
integers, $0 \le e \le f$, and $e^2+f^2 = \frac{(q-p)^2 + (q+p)^2}{4} =
\frac{2p^2+2q^2}{4} = m^2$. Conversely if $e^2+f^2 = m^2$ then
$p = f-e \ge 0$, $q = f+e \ge p$ satisfy $p^2+q^2 = 2(e^2+f^2) = 2m^2$. The
two maps are inverse by direct substitution. Finally $m^2 - p^2 =
e^2+f^2-(f-e)^2 = 2ef$ and $q^2 - m^2 = (f+e)^2 - e^2 - f^2 = 2ef$, so
$(p^2, m^2, q^2)$ is an AP with difference $2ef$. ∎

**Definition F2.3 (congrua).** The *congrua of* $m$ are
$$D(m) \;=\; \{\,2ef \;:\; e^2+f^2 = m^2,\ e,f > 0\,\} .$$
(These are the classical *congrua* with square "midpoint": $d \in D(m)$ iff
$m^2 - d,\, m^2,\, m^2 + d$ are all perfect squares with $d > 0$, by F2.2.
Note $e = f$ is impossible, since $2e^2 = m^2$ forces $e = 0$ by the
irrationality of $\sqrt2$; and $e = 0$ gives the trivial AP $d = 0$.
Equivalently: $D(m) = \{4\cdot\text{area} : \text{right triangle with
integer legs and hypotenuse } m\}$.)

**Lemma F2.4 (congrua are faithful).** Distinct proper decompositions give
distinct congrua: if $e^2+f^2 = e'^2+f'^2 = m^2$ with $0 < e \le f$,
$0 < e' \le f'$ and $2ef = 2e'f'$, then $(e,f) = (e',f')$. *Proof.* From
equal sums of squares and equal products, $(e+f)^2 = (e'+f')^2$ and
$(f-e)^2 = (f'-e')^2$; all quantities nonnegative, so $e+f = e'+f'$ and
$f-e = f'-e'$, hence equality. ∎ Consequently $|D(m)| = |\mathcal{P}(m)| - 1$
(discarding the trivial $(0,m)$).

## 2. The four-congrua reformulation

**Theorem F2.5.** An admissible magic square of squares exists **iff** there
are $m \ge 1$ and integers $u, v$ with the distinctness condition of F1.3
such that
$$|u|,\ |v|,\ |u+v|,\ |u-v| \;\in\; D(m).$$
The square is then $L(m^2, u, v)$ and its magic sum is $3m^2$.

*Proof.* By F1.2 an MSS3 is $L(c,u,v)$ with all nine entries square; the
center $c$ is an entry, so $c = m^2$. For each offset
$w \in \{u, v, u+v, u-v\}$, the entries $m^2 - w$ and $m^2 + w$ are squares,
i.e. $(m^2-|w|, m^2, m^2+|w|)$ is an AP of squares with difference
$|w| > 0$ (nonzero by F1.3), i.e. $|w| \in D(m)$ by F2.2/F2.3. Conversely,
given such $m, u, v$, all nine entries of $L(m^2,u,v)$ are squares and
distinct by F1.3. ∎

**Remark (the four offsets are four *distinct* congrua).** F1.3 excludes
$u = \pm v$, $uv = 0$, $u = \pm 2v$, $v = \pm 2u$; a short case check (done
mechanically in the verify script) shows these exclusions make
$|u|, |v|, |u+v|, |u-v|$ pairwise distinct. So an MSS3 needs **at least four
distinct congrua of one hypotenuse $m$, linked by the additive relations**
$$d_3 = d_1 + d_2, \qquad d_4 = |d_1 - d_2| \qquad (d_1 = |u|,\ d_2 = |v|,\ \text{after signs/WLOG}).$$

This is the formulation attacked in
[A3](../attacks/A3-simultaneous-congrua.md).

## 3. Counting decompositions: the Gaussian integers

Classical, included in full because [F2.7] and several attacks depend on it.

**Lemma F2.6a ($\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a UFD).** For
$\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$, $\beta \ne 0$, write
$\alpha/\beta = x + yi$ with $x,y \in \mathbb{Q}$ and pick integers $x_0,y_0$
with $|x-x_0| \le \tfrac12$, $|y-y_0| \le \tfrac12$; then
$\rho = \alpha - \beta(x_0+y_0i)$ satisfies
$N(\rho) = N(\beta)\,((x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2) \le \tfrac12 N(\beta) < N(\beta)$,
where $N(a+bi) = a^2+b^2$ is multiplicative. Existence of factorization into
irreducibles follows by strong induction on the norm; uniqueness follows from
Euclid's lemma, which follows from Bézout, which follows from the Euclidean
property (the ideal $(\alpha,\beta)$ is generated by any nonzero element of
minimal norm). Units are exactly $N=1$: $\{\pm1, \pm i\}$. ∎

**Lemma F2.6b (Fermat's two-square theorem & the splitting of primes).**
Let $p$ be an odd rational prime.
1. If $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ then $-1$ is a quadratic residue mod $p$:
   by Wilson's theorem $(p-1)! \equiv -1$, and pairing $k$ with $-k$ gives
   $(p-1)! \equiv (-1)^{(p-1)/2}\big(((p-1)/2)!\big)^2 \equiv
   \big(((p-1)/2)!\big)^2 \pmod p$. So $p \mid s^2+1 = (s+i)(s-i)$ for
   $s = ((p-1)/2)!$; since $p$ divides neither factor in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$
   (their imaginary parts are $\pm1$), $p$ is not prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$,
   so $p = \pi\bar\pi$ with $N(\pi) = p$, i.e. $p = a^2+b^2$. The factors
   $\pi, \bar\pi$ are non-associate (an associate equality forces
   $a = \pm b$ or $ab = 0$, impossible for $a^2+b^2 = p$ odd prime).
2. If $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ then $p$ stays prime ("inert"): $a^2+b^2 \equiv
   0 \pmod p$ with $(a,b) \not\equiv (0,0)$ would make $-1$ a QR mod $p$,
   contradicting Euler's criterion
   $(-1)^{(p-1)/2} = -1$. So $N(\pi) = p$ is impossible and any
   factorization of $p$ is trivial.
3. $2 = -i(1+i)^2$ ramifies; $1+i$ is prime ($N = 2$). ∎

**Theorem F2.6 (number of proper decompositions of $m^2$).** Write
$m = 2^{a_0} \prod_i p_i^{a_i} \prod_j q_j^{b_j}$ with $p_i \equiv 1$,
$q_j \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. Then
$$\#\{(e,f) : 0 < e \le f,\ e^2+f^2 = m^2\} \;=\;
\frac{\prod_i (2a_i + 1)\; -\; 1}{2}
\;=\; |D(m)|.$$

*Proof.* Solutions of $e^2+f^2 = m^2$ correspond to elements
$z = e+fi \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$ of norm $m^2$. By unique factorization and the
splitting above, $z = \iota\, (1+i)^{2a_0} \prod_i \pi_i^{r_i}
\bar\pi_i^{2a_i - r_i} \prod_j q_j^{b_j}$ with a unit $\iota \in
\{\pm1,\pm i\}$ and $0 \le r_i \le 2a_i$ (the $q_j$-exponent must be exactly
$b_j$ in conjugate pairs since $q_j$ is prime of norm $q_j^2$; the norm
forces each exponent as stated). So the number of *ordered, signed* pairs is
$4\prod_i(2a_i+1)$. Exactly $4$ of them have $ef = 0$ (namely
$z = \pm m, \pm mi$). Each unordered positive pair $\{e,f\}$ with
$0 < e < f$ arises from exactly $8$ such $z$ ($4$ units $\times$ conjugation;
$e = f$ never occurs, F2.3). Hence the count is
$(4\prod(2a_i+1) - 4)/8 = (\prod(2a_i+1)-1)/2$, and it equals $|D(m)|$ by
F2.4. ∎

**Corollary F2.7 (center constraint — PROVEN).** If an MSS3 has center
$m^2$, then $\prod_{p \equiv 1 (4),\, p^a \| m}(2a+1) \ge 9$. In particular
$m$ is divisible by at least one prime $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and **either** by
two distinct such primes **or** by $p^4$ for one such prime. The smallest
$m$ with four distinct congrua is $m = 65 = 5\cdot13$.

*Proof.* By F2.5 + the Remark, $D(m)$ contains four distinct elements, so
$|D(m)| = (\prod(2a_i+1)-1)/2 \ge 4$. ∎

*(Honesty note: assertions in the search literature that the center needs
**three** distinct primes $\equiv 1 \bmod 4$ use the additive relations, not
just counting; we do not rely on them and revisit the question in
[A3](../attacks/A3-simultaneous-congrua.md).)*

## 4. The Gaussian form of the whole problem

**Theorem F2.8.** An MSS3 with center $m^2$ exists **iff** there are
$z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4 \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$, each of norm $N(z_k) = m^2$, with

$$\operatorname{Im}(z_3^2) = \operatorname{Im}(z_1^2) + \operatorname{Im}(z_2^2),
\qquad
\operatorname{Im}(z_4^2) = \operatorname{Im}(z_1^2) - \operatorname{Im}(z_2^2),$$

and $\operatorname{Im}(z_1^2), \operatorname{Im}(z_2^2)$ subject to the
nondegeneracy of F1.3 (via $u = \operatorname{Im}(z_1^2)$,
$v = \operatorname{Im}(z_2^2)$).

*Proof.* For $z = e+fi$, $\operatorname{Im}(z^2) = 2ef$, and $N(z) = m^2$
says $(|e|,|f|)$ is a decomposition of $m^2$; signs of $e,f$ give
$\operatorname{Im}(z^2)$ either sign. Apply F2.5 with $u =
\operatorname{Im}(z_1^2)$, $v = \operatorname{Im}(z_2^2)$, $u + v =
\operatorname{Im}(z_3^2)$, $u - v = \operatorname{Im}(z_4^2)$; conversely
read decompositions off an MSS3. ∎

**Attack surface.** On the "circle" $\{z \in \mathbb{Z}[i] : N(z) = m^2\}$
— a finite set of $4\prod(2a_i+1)$ points governed by the factorization of
$m$ — the problem asks for a nontrivial additive coincidence among the
finitely many values $\operatorname{Im}(z^2)$. All global structure of the
problem lives in how these values interact additively as $m$ varies. This
viewpoint feeds [A3](../attacks/A3-simultaneous-congrua.md) (statistics and
elliptic curves) and [A2](../attacks/A2-function-field.md) (the polynomial
analogue).
